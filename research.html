Corals that count: Valuing carbon stocks and biomass cumulation in a Floating Reef Device

Giovanni Giallongo, Mattia Galante, Silvia Ferrini, Nadav Shashar, Baruch Rinkevich · under review

abstract

Coral reef degradation threatens biodiversity, ecosystem functioning and global livelihoods. Yet, rising costs limit restoration efforts. Here we assess whether coral restoration infrastructure can deliver socio-economic benefits that justify investment beyond direct financial returns. Using the model Floating Reef Device (FRD), a coral-restoration infrastructure that promotes coral growth and reef-associated communities, we propose an interdisciplinary framework integrating ecological modelling, ecosystem-services valuation, and cost-benefit analysis. Two key ecosystem services generated by the FRDs, carbon storage and fish biomass enhancement, were quantified and assessed for economic values under three management scenarios, differing in coral extraction intensity. Over a ten-year period, a 100 m² FRD-module stored to 708 kg carbon, simultaneously generating substantial fish biomass valued to 22,857US$ annually. Scenarios identified trade-offs between maximizing carbon turnover and maintaining habitat stability: intensive coral harvesting increased carbon sequestration but reduced habitat complexity and associated fish biomass. Financial analysis showed that all scenarios remain privately unprofitable under current cost structure (FNPV4% ranging -67,623 to -134,199-US$). However, when incorporating ecosystem services, economic analysis revealed positive net societal benefits for the no-extraction and partial extraction scenarios (ENPV3% = 72,648US$ and 39,165US$, respectively). These results indicate that coral-restoration infrastructure can generate societal welfare benefits even when not financially viable for private investors. Additional revenues, including tourism, coral aquaculture, education, and bioprospecting, may help close this gap. Overall, FRDs function as multifunctional socio-ecological assets, linking biodiversity, climate, and economic benefits, while this framework supports their integration into natural-capital accounting and ocean-finance mechanisms.

Consumer preferences for finfish attributes in Europe: A meta-regression of stated-choice evidence and implications for policy

Mattia Galante, Gaetano Grilli, Javier Cantillo, Silvia Ferrini · under review

abstract

Fish is often referred to as an alternative to other animal-based foods for sustainable and secure food chains. Although its lower carbon footprint and recent developments suggest that it is possible to increase fish production within ecological boundaries, the sector faces several environmental challenges including pressure on resources, stocks depletion and pollution. To address these issues, it is essential to detect consumers’ attitudes towards sustainable seafood. A meta-regression analysis of 18 discrete choice experiments explores the willingness-to-pay of European consumers for the characteristics of finfish products. Our main finding is that characteristics like origin, production source, species have greater importance than sustainability certifications. In general, results show variability in preferences and detect positive attitudes towards domestic, wild-caught finfish. The uncertainty surrounding processed products may create opportunities for streamlining industrial processes. These and other results are discussed and policy implications provided.

Cost vector effects and familiarity in discrete choice experiments

Mattia Galante, Carlo Fezzi, Sandra Notaro, Silvia Ferrini · presented at WCERE 2026, IAERE 2026 and the EAAE–EAERE Seminar 2025

abstract

Discrete choice experiments (DCEs) are used to estimate willingness-to-pay (WTP) for environmental attributes, but derived welfare measures may be sensitive to experimental design when preferences are uncertain and therefore dependent on contextual cues. A prominent concern is the cost vector effect, whereby WTP increases with the range of the price attribute presented in choice tasks. We hypothesize that familiarity with the valuation context mitigates this bias by stabilizing preferences. To test this, we designed a 2 × 2 split-sample DCE valuing recreational access to temperate beaches with different attributes. An online sample of respondents was stratified into experienced users (divers/snorkellers) and non-experienced users (occasional beach visitors) and randomly assigned to one of two price vectors. Data were analysed using an Error-Component Mixed Logit model in WTP space, estimated via weighted maximum likelihood to reflect individual exposure to coastal recreation. Results show strong evidence of a cost vector effect among non-experienced users, whose WTP estimates are systematically and significantly higher under the high-price treatment for most attributes, especially for high ecosystem quality, natural reefs, and low congestion. In contrast, experienced users’ WTP is statistically stable across price vectors for all attributes, indicating robust preferences. The study concludes by providing useful recommendations for experimental design.
